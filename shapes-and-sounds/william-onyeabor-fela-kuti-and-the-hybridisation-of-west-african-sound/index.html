William Onyeabor, Fela Kuti and the Hybridisation of West African Sound

By: Mimi Belilty

The dynamic exchange of sound sits at the heart of musical hybridisation, a process both enabled and complicated by globalisation. Cultural exchange in music is never one-directional; it is reciprocal, fluid and often unequal. Non-western musicians have long incorporated European electronic equipment and American Western Swing into local tradition, contributing to the expansion of global music markets. Yet, this exchange has rarely occurred on neutral terms.

Artists from colonised or formerly colonised regions have often been expected to modernise their local music through the adoption of Western aesthetics, technologies and structures – all while attempting to preserve cultural identity and musical integrity. This pressure reflects a longer history of musical colonialism, enacted not only through political power but also by musicians, critics, producers, ethnomusicologists and musicologists. In many cases, sound has been stripped, reshaped and hybridised, to suit western consumption and succession.

The commodification of non-Western music continues this legacy. Through Western- dominated streaming platforms, record labels and markets, music is frequently reproduced and exoticised to sound more ‘oriental’, ‘authentic’ or ‘different’, aligning with Western expectations of cultural otherness. These reproductions are often flattened into the umbrella genre World Music, grouped together on a single shelf or playlist regardless of context, history or difference.

Genres like Afrobeat, Son, Reggae, Highlife and Carnatic music emerged through complex processes of cultural exchange, shaped by colonialism, resistance and innovation. Today, they play a crucial role in reclaiming of non-Western sound within contemporary contexts. In the digital age, musical exchange between the West and non-West have accelerated further, raising ongoing questions around authorship. Ownership, power and cultural survival.

William Onyeabor actively hybridised and exchanged musical techniques between the US and Nigeria, diverging sharply from the dominant West African styles of his time. While Afrobeat – a mix of traditional Yoruba, Jazz, Funk and Fuji – alongside Ghanaian Highlife and trad-local sound shaped much of the region’s sound. He is widely regarded as one of the first musicians in West Africa (arguably nationally)  to gain access to a wide range of electronic, acoustic and early digital instruments.

His carefully curated equipment included synthisers, drum machines, vinyl pressing machines and distinctive recording devices such as an Octave the Cat, a Minikorg 700 and a Polymoog Ella Rhapsody 610. These instruments would have been sourced and shipped from Western markets, both reflecting access and intent. Working from Enugu, Nigeria, Onyeabor built his own recording studio and independently produced his work. In 1978, he released an album featuring his most recognised track, ‘Atomic Bomb’, a record that fused machine-driven sound with Nigerian rhythmic structures in a way that felt unprecedented at the time.

A contemporary of Onyeabor was fellow Nigerian musician Fela Kuti. Although there are no documented relationships between the two artists, Kuti captivated the nation and later the word, becoming known as the father of Afrobeat and placing Western African sound firmly on the global stage. Unlike Onyeabor, Kuti made little use of synthesizers or advanced studio technology. Instead, he relied on combo organs and electric piano as an imperial duo, to approximate the metallic, otherworldly tones associated with synthesisers. His approach remained rooted in organic instrumentation and live ensemble performance, contrasting starkly with Onyeabor’s technological divergence from traditional Afrobeat techniques.

Despite these differences, Western musical influence was central to both artists’ practices. Onyeabor embraced Western recording technology to reshape perceptions of Nigerian sound, whilst Kuti enraged directly with Western musicians through collaborations with artists such as Talking Heads and Ginger Baker, to create songs like “Lets Start ” [Kuti ft Ginger Baker]1. Their contrasting approaches highlight how Nigerian artists developed a distinct response to Western influence, one through technological adoption, the other through political collaboration and stylistic adaptation.

Onyearbor emerged as a central yet elusive figure of his era, blending Western equipment with traditional Nigerian rhythms to create a hybrid genre that continues to inspire contemporary musicians. Musical theorist, Simon Frith suggests that World Music did not emerge directly from global pop, but rather it “roots rock […] made up by British and American bands who remained true to the rock and roll’s original spirit. This was music for grown-ups not adolescents, unashamedly functional (for dancing, courting, expressive of local community, emotionally robust”2. This framing resonates with Onyeabor’s later Western reception.

Often labeled the “Synth Cowboy”3, Onyeabor adopted an Americana- inspired aesthetic that subverted the dominant image of 1970s American rock musicians. Although largely underground during his lifetime, his work has since been embraced by audiences (both Western and non-Western) drawn to experimental production, alternative synth textures and Afro-funk futurism. Frith’s notion of hybridisation through “roots rock”4 aligns with a broader movement of non-western artists striving to remain grounded in local sound while simultaneously engaging global audiences.

“Cultural exchange in music is never one-directional; it is reciprocal, fluid and often unequal”

In 2014, the Western record label Luaka Bop released the short documentary, Fantastic Man,5 with the intention of locating and interviewing William Onyeabor. The film frames his musical and cultural significance largely through the voices of Western producers, collectors, writers and musicians who followed his work from the 1970s onwards. Whilst presented as recovery and celebration, it reflects a colonial impulse to discover, narrate and legitimise non-Western sound through Western mediation.

The speculative history surrounding Onyeabor comes to a halt when the film crew track him down in Enugu. At the film’s climax, Onyeabor declines an interview. Instead, his heavily synthesised track Anything you Sow6 fades in as the camera enters his home. His synthesisers sit beside Christian iconography, paintings of Jesus Christ and personal photographs, presented almost shrine-like. In refusing to speak, Onyeabor denies the documentary its desired narrative ownership, asserting control over his life and work are represented. His refusal to perform authenticity for a Western gaze reinforces this position — resisting extraction, categorisation, and ultimately, commodification.

Words by: Mimi Belilty (b. 2002) is a British-Moroccan artist, music researcher, broadcaster, and writer based in Glasgow. Her interdisciplinary work explores cultural sources from the Middle East, Africa, and her own familial archives, shaped by experiences of diaspora and grief. This writing grows out of wider research into Afrobeat and synthesis, where sustained listening becomes both a method of inquiry and a personal point of connection. She suggests listening to The Moon and The Sun after reading.
